A lighthouse is a tower, building, or other type of structure designed to emit light from a system of lamps and lenses and to serve as a beacon for navigational aid for pilots at sea or on inland waterways.[1] Lighthouses mark dangerous coastlines, hazardous shoals, reefs, rocks, and safe entries to harbors.

The number of lighthouses in use has declined because of the expense of maintenance and the arrival of electronic navigational systems.[2] Many former lighthouses are now museums or private homes.

History[edit]

Before the development of clearly defined ports, mariners were guided by fires built on hilltops. Since raising the fire would improve its visibility, placing the fire on a platform became a practice that led to the development of the lighthouse.[3]

In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the spread of trade by sea led to a large building program. Engineers designed towers that could survive storms on exposed rocks, and lens makers developed systems that bent the light into a narrow, powerful beam.

Construction[edit]

The main parts of a lighthouse are the tower, the lantern room at the top, and the light itself. A gallery around the lantern room gives the keeper access for cleaning the windows.

Modern lights are usually automated and powered by mains electricity or by solar panels with batteries. Keepers were withdrawn from most stations by the end of the twentieth century.

Notable lighthouses[edit]

  • The Pharos of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.
  • The Tower of Hercules, still in use after almost two thousand years.
  • The Eddystone Lighthouse, rebuilt four times on the same reef.
