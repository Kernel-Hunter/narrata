The Slow Return of the Night Train

Sleeper services were written off as relics. Now new routes are filling up, and the people running them are learning old lessons again.

By Ines Okafor · 12 minute read

At ten past nine on a Thursday evening, the platform at the central station is crowded with people who do not look like they are going anywhere in a hurry. A couple shares a paper bag of pastries. A man in a suit has already changed into soft shoes. A family with two sleepy children counts their bags for the third time, while the conductor walks the length of the train with a clipboard and a patient smile.

The night train to the coast leaves at nine thirty and arrives a little after seven the next morning. It is not fast. A plane would cover the same distance in under two hours, and a car would do it in eight. But nobody on this platform seems to be thinking about speed. They are thinking about the small cabin with the folded blanket, the window that will show nothing but dark fields and the occasional lit village, and the strange pleasure of waking up somewhere else.

For most of the last thirty years, services like this one were treated as a problem to be managed. Carriages aged, timetables thinned out, and route after route was quietly withdrawn. The arithmetic seemed simple: sleeper cars carry fewer passengers than seated ones, they need staff overnight, and they spend the day parked in a siding earning nothing.

A business that runs at night

The arithmetic has not changed, but the people doing it have. Marta Lindqvist, who manages the operator's overnight routes, keeps a printed chart on the wall of her office. It shows every cabin on every train for the next three months, coloured by how many have been sold. In the summer most of the chart is dark red.

"We used to think of the night train as a hotel that happens to move," she says. "That was the mistake. People do not book it for the bed. They book it because it turns travel time into sleep time, and because they arrive in the middle of a city instead of an airport forty minutes outside it."

The economics still depend on using the carriages twice. A train that arrives at seven is cleaned, restocked and turned around by early evening, ready to go back. Lindqvist's team spends much of its time on that turnaround: how many sheets fit in a laundry cart, how long it takes to reset a cabin, and which station has a siding long enough to keep the train out of the way during the day.

People do not book it for the bed. They book it because it turns travel time into sleep time.

Old lessons

Some of what the new operators are learning was common knowledge in 1960. Passengers sleep better when the train does not stop and start through the night, so timetables now include long, slow stretches where the train simply rolls at a steady pace instead of hurrying to wait at a junction. Breakfast matters more than dinner. A cabin that locks from the inside matters more than a cabin with a view.

Other lessons are new. Booking systems built for seats struggled with cabins that can be sold whole or by the berth. Families wanted connecting doors. Travellers with bicycles, which had been banned for years, turned out to be some of the most loyal customers, so two carriages now carry racks instead of a dining table.

The conductor on the Thursday train, Samuel Reyes, has worked overnight services for nineteen years. He remembers when a full train was rare. "Now I get complaints that we are sold out," he says, and laughs. "It is a nice complaint to have. But it means people who could not get a ticket take the plane, and that is the opposite of what we want."

What comes next

The obstacle now is not demand but equipment. New sleeper carriages take years to design and build, and only a handful of factories make them. Several operators are refurbishing older cars while they wait, stripping them down to the frame and fitting new walls, wiring and washrooms. It is slow work, and the finished carriages are heavier and less efficient than modern designs.

There is also the question of the border. A night train that crosses into a neighbouring country needs agreements about crews, tracks and timetables, and each country has its own rules. The most popular new routes are the ones that cross two or three borders, which is exactly what makes them hard to run.

Back on the platform, the conductor blows his whistle at nine twenty nine. The doors close with a soft hiss. Inside, the family has found its cabin, and the children are already arguing about who gets the top bunk. The train pulls out slowly, past the depot and the dark warehouses, and settles into the long rhythm it will keep until morning.
